# Overview

Cognitive dissonance (Leon Festinger) is the tension that arises when two cognitions conflict — typically **what you believe** vs **what you do** (e.g. “smoking harms me” while lighting another cigarette). The psyche prefers coherence; when coherence breaks, you feel pressure to restore it.

People rarely sit in that discomfort for long. They change the behavior, change the belief, add a justifying story, or avoid the topic. Seeing *which move you default to* is the self-knowledge payoff: it explains stubborn habits and post-hoc rationalizations after hard choices.

### Diagram

```text
  Belief  <-->  Behavior (clash)
         \    /
        discomfort → change act | change belief | justify | avoid
```

# When to use

- You keep doing something you “know” is wrong, wasteful, or misaligned.
- After a decision you feel oddly compelled to defend it beyond the evidence.
- You notice special pleading: “just this once,” “everyone does it,” “the studies are unclear.”
- You want to understand loyalty to a failing plan, brand, or identity.

Skip it when the error is a *thinking distortion about facts* more than belief–action clash (use [Cognitive Distortion](/models/understand-self/cognitive-distortion.md)), or when the choice type itself is unclear (use [Hard Choice Paradox](/models/choose/hard-choice-paradox.md)).

# How it works

### The conflict

Two (or more) inconsistent elements — attitudes, values, actions, new information — create dissonance. Intensity rises with:

- How important the belief or identity is
- How hard the inconsistent action is to undo
- How public or irreversible the commitment was

### Typical resolutions

1. **Change behavior** — quit, apologize, reverse course (often hardest short-term).
2. **Change belief** — downgrade the original value or “update” what counts as true.
3. **Add consonant cognition** — invent reasons that make the conflict make sense (“I need it for stress”; “quality over quantity”).
4. **Avoid / deny** — stop looking at evidence; surround yourself with confirming voices.

### Diagnostic questions

1. What do I claim to value — and what does my calendar / wallet / mouth actually do?
2. After the last big choice, did I seek disconfirming info — or only reassurance?
3. Which resolution am I using: real change, story, or avoidance?
4. If I dropped the justifying story, what would I have to feel or do?

### Practice tip

Name the two colliding sentences in writing (“I believe X” / “I do Y”). Then choose *consciously* among the four resolutions — especially when the default is #3 or #4.

### Facilitation steps

1. **Conflict** — Name the belief/value and the behavior (or two beliefs) that clash.
2. **Discomfort** — How does the tension show up (rationalizing, avoidance, irritability)?
3. **Default move** — Do you usually change behavior, change belief, add a story, or avoid?
4. **Choice** — Which resolution do you *want* this time (not the automatic one)?
5. **Next act** — What concrete action aligns with that chosen resolution?
