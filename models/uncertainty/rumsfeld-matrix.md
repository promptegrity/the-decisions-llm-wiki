# Overview

The Rumsfeld Matrix maps knowledge and ignorance into four boxes: **known knowns**, **known unknowns**, **unknown knowns**, and **unknown unknowns**. The point is not to eliminate uncertainty — it is to see *which kind* you face so you plan, research, or stay humble accordingly.

Named after Donald Rumsfeld’s public distinction (2002) between things we know we know, things we know we don’t know, and things we don’t know we don’t know.

### Diagram

```text
                 Known                    Unknown
              +------------------------+------------------------+
  Known       | Known knowns           | Known unknowns         |
              | (facts you can use)    | (questions to research)|
              +------------------------+------------------------+
  Unknown     | (blind confidence)     | Unknown unknowns       |
              |                        | (hunt carefully)       |
              +------------------------+------------------------+
```

# When to use

- You are assessing risk in a project, market, or relationship and the list of “risks” feels incomplete.
- A plan assumes the world will behave like the past.
- Stakeholders disagree because some treat unknowns as if they were known.
- You need a shared language for “we don’t know yet” vs “we don’t even know what to ask.”

Skip it when you already have a concrete failure chain to analyze (use [Swiss Cheese](/models/uncertainty/swiss-cheese-model.md)) or when the issue is rare extreme events that break your models (use [Black Swan](/models/uncertainty/black-swan-theory.md)).

# How it works

Draw (or imagine) a 2×2 of **awareness** × **knowledge**:

| | **Known** | **Unknown** |
|--|-----------|-------------|
| **Known (aware)** | **Known knowns** — facts, skills, confirmed data you can act on | **Known unknowns** — open questions you can research, measure, or insure against |
| **Unknown (unaware)** | **Unknown knowns** — tacit knowledge, blind spots, forgotten lessons, things others know that you don’t | **Unknown unknowns** — risks and opportunities outside your frame; you cannot list them yet |

### Questions to place each item

1. **Do we know this?** Is there evidence, or only assumption?
2. **Do we know that we know / don’t know?** Are we aware of the gap, or is it invisible to us?
3. **Who else might know?** Colleagues, competitors, customers, history — can turn unknown knowns into knowns.

### How to act by quadrant

1. **Known knowns** — use them; document and share so they stay known.
2. **Known unknowns** — turn into experiments, sensors, reserves, or explicit assumptions to test.
3. **Unknown knowns** — surface via feedback, outsiders, postmortems, and asking “what are we taking for granted?”
4. **Unknown unknowns** — you cannot checklist them; build resilience, optionality, and early-warning culture instead of false precision.

### Practice tip

In a risk workshop, force the group to fill *known unknowns* first (honest gaps), then hunt for *unknown knowns* (what veterans or critics see that the plan ignores). Treat *unknown unknowns* as a reason for buffers — not as a blank to invent fake certainty.

### Facilitation steps

1. **Domain** — What risk or decision domain are we mapping?
2. **Known knowns** — What do we know and can act on?
3. **Known unknowns** — What do we know we don’t know (and should investigate)?
4. **Unknown unknowns** — Where might we be blind (and how do we hunt carefully)?
5. **Plan** — One action per filled cell that matters this week?
